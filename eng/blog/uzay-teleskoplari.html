Looking Beyond the Sky: Why Do We Need Space Telescopes?

Aslı Bolat · Astronomer & Educator · July 26, 2026 · 4 min read

When we look up at the night sky, we see the mesmerizing light of thousands of stars. But from an astronomer's point of view, that view comes with a serious obstacle: even the most advanced telescopes on Earth can't observe the universe in full, because of the effects of our planet's atmosphere.

So why are billions of dollars invested in projects like Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope? Why send telescopes into the harsh conditions of space instead of leaving them on Earth? The main reason is simple: to observe the universe more clearly, more deeply, and across wavelengths we simply can't see from the ground.

1. The Atmosphere Blurs Images

Earth's atmosphere is constantly moving. Temperature changes, air currents and shifts in density bend the light coming from stars as it passes through — which is exactly why stars appear to "twinkle" in the sky.

Ground-based telescopes are affected by this atmospheric turbulence, while space telescopes operate completely outside the atmosphere and collect light without any distortion. The result: much sharper, higher-resolution images.

2. The Atmosphere Hides Most of the Universe

Our atmosphere is a natural shield that protects life from harmful solar radiation. But that protection is a serious disadvantage for astronomers — because objects in the universe emit radiation not only in visible light, but also in ultraviolet, infrared, X-ray and gamma-ray wavelengths.

Most of this radiation is absorbed by the atmosphere and can't be observed from Earth. The James Webb Space Telescope, for example, uses infrared observations to study star-forming regions and the universe's first galaxies, while X-ray telescopes reveal valuable information about black holes, neutron stars and supernova remnants.

3. Uninterrupted Observation

Ground-based telescopes are affected by many natural obstacles — cloudy weather, rain, atmospheric conditions and daylight. Space telescopes are largely free of these limitations. Positioned in the right orbit, they can observe continuously for long periods and collect extremely precise data.

Space telescopes weren't built just to take prettier pictures. They are scientific laboratories that observe the universe from beyond Earth's boundaries, making visible the light we otherwise can't see. Much of what we know today — from the structure of black holes to the birth of stars, from the formation of galaxies to the earliest moments of the universe — comes from data gathered by space telescopes.

As technology advances, the next generation of telescopes sent into space will keep shedding light on the universe's unanswered questions. Perhaps the greatest discovery of the future won't come from Earth at all — but from beyond the sky.
